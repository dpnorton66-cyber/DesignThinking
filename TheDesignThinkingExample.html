The Levreau Decision

The situation

Maison Levreau makes the Alcine bag to order, in the boutique and online.

Last year the range was extended. Three sizes, seven leathers, four hardware finishes and twelve linings now give 1,008 combinations, up from 240.

Since then, completed orders through the configurator have fallen from 41 per cent of sessions to 19 per cent. Boutique appointments run twenty minutes longer than they used to. Clients keep saying the same thing to advisors, and the advisors keep writing it in their notes: there is too much to choose from, and they cannot tell which combinations go together.

Nobody has said what to do about it. The executive committee wants it addressed within 16 working days.

You will work through five stages in order: empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test. One decision at each, with four routes open to you every time. The solution is not in the brief, because there is no solution in the brief.

Stage by stage: what you did, and what the stage is for
The five stages, and where test sends you back to
  1. Empathise. Understand the people you are designing for by observing them and spending time in their situation. Record what you find as pains and gains, and mark which were observed and which were only stated.
  2. Define. Synthesise what you saw into one point of view: who, what they need, and the insight behind it. Written as a "how might we" statement.
  3. Ideate. Generate a wide range of options so you get past the obvious ones. Judge them afterwards, against the statement.
  4. Prototype. Turn an idea into something people can experience, cheaply enough that you can afford to be wrong.
  5. Test. Try it with the people who have the problem, observe, and use what you learn to refine the prototype and revisit your understanding.

The stages are not a one-way street

Test does not end the process. What you learn there sends you back: to prototype if the idea is right and the execution is wrong, to ideate if the idea is wrong, and to empathise if the statement itself was wrong. This simulation runs the loop once so that it fits in a workshop. Real work goes round it several times.

Three tests for a "how might we"

  • No solution inside it. If the statement names the answer, the team has nothing left to do.
  • Narrow enough to act on. If it would justify any option, it will not help you choose between them.
  • Traceable to an observed pain or gain. Point at the line on the wall it came from.

The answer already exists in the trade. A seasonal edit, a trunk show, a window: every house presents a chosen selection and keeps the rest of the range behind it. When the maison moved the range online it published the grid and left out the edit, which was the part doing the work.
